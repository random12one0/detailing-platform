# What we send, and what we don't

**For Andrew. Written to be talked through, not to be agreed with.**

This is not a plan and it does not recommend anything. It is the four things
you need in front of you before deciding what a detailing business should send
its customers: what goes out today, where nothing goes out at all, what the
law asks for, and the questions that are yours to answer.

Where something is uncertain, it says so.

---

## 1. The short version, in case you read nothing else

**Email works. Texting does not — not the way you probably think it does.**

**There are seventeen kinds of email.** Thirteen can reach a customer, three
come to you, and one goes to someone you add to your team. They are all listed
in the next section; if you count that table, you should get seventeen.

**Most go out by themselves.** Four only ever go because you did something: an
invoice, a membership link, a team invitation, and a campaign. Those are
counted too — they are still messages your business sends.

**There is also a third channel you may not be counting: thirteen kinds of
phone alert.** Every one of them is for **you**, not for a customer — they are
how the product taps you on the shoulder. They are listed in section 2.

The product sends **no texts at all.** What it has instead is a button that
opens the messaging app on *your phone*, with the message already typed, and
you press send yourself. The text then comes from your personal number, lands
in your personal sent messages, and the product never knows it happened.

That is worth sitting with, because most of the decisions below hang off it.
A text from your phone is legally *your* message. A text from the platform
would be *ours*, and a different and much stricter set of rules would apply.

## 2. What the product sends today

**Seventeen in total: thirteen to a customer, three to you, one to a team
member.** Count the rows — the number above is only worth having if it matches
them.

### To the customer — thirteen

| What it is | What sets it off | On or off to start with |
|---|---|---|
| **Booking confirmed** | They book, or you accept their request | **On** |
| **Request received** | They ask for a time you have to approve | **On** |
| **Request answered** | You accept or decline that request | **On** |
| **Appointment reminder** | A set number of hours before the job — two hours to start with | **On** |
| **A second reminder** | A second, separate nudge before the same job | **Off** |
| **Moved** | The booking's date or time changes | **On** |
| **Cancelled** | The booking is cancelled | **On** |
| **Follow-up after the job** | After the work is finished | **On** |
| **Invoice** | You send one | On when you send it |
| **Membership link** | They join or manage a plan | On when it happens |
| **Membership cancelled** | A plan ends | On when it happens |
| **"Your maintenance is due"** | A coating or service reaches its due date | **Off** — and this one is different; see below |
| **A campaign** | Only when you write one and choose who gets it | On when you send it |

### To you — three

| What it is | What sets it off | On or off to start with |
|---|---|---|
| **New booking** | A customer books | **On** |
| **Your own day-before reminder** | Before a job you're doing | **On** |
| **Still waiting** | A request has sat unanswered | **On** |

### To a team member — one

**An invitation**, when you add someone to your business.

**That is 13 + 3 + 1 = 17.** The platform's own emails to you about billing
and system alerts are not in that number, and section 7 says why.

**What that seventeen is counting, so nobody re-counts it and gives you a
different answer.** It counts **messages as a person would meet them** — a
booking confirmation and a "we got your request" are two different messages
arriving in two different situations, so they are two. Underneath, the code
has **fourteen** of these written out, because a few are one piece of writing
used in two situations. Fourteen is the right number if you are counting work
to be done. **Seventeen is the right number if you are deciding what your
business sends, which is what this page is for.**

### Push notifications — thirteen, and every single one is for you

**This is a whole channel that is easy to forget, because no customer ever
sees any of it.** These are the alerts that pop up on your phone or laptop.
**None of them reaches a customer. Not one.**

They are **on to start with** — all sixteen businesses on the system have them
switched on — but they only actually arrive once you have allowed
notifications on a particular device. No device, no alert.

| What pops up | What sets it off |
|---|---|
| **New booking** | A customer books straight in |
| **Booking request** | A customer asks for a time you have to approve |
| **Quote accepted** | A customer accepts a quote you sent |
| **Booking cancelled** | A booking is cancelled |
| **Booking rescheduled** | A booking moves |
| **Booking updated** | A booking changes some other way |
| **Starting soon** | A job is about to begin |
| **Wrapping up soon** | A job is about to finish |
| **Upcoming job reminder** | Ahead of a job |
| **Still waiting on you** | A request has been sitting unanswered |
| **Finalize payment** | A job is done and the money is not settled |
| **Payment finalized** | It has been settled |
| **"Today: 3 jobs"** | A daily summary of what is on |

**Thirteen messages from twelve places in the product** — one of those places
sends either "New booking" or "Booking request" depending on which it was.

### The one that is genuinely different

**"Your maintenance is due"** is the only message here that *sells something*.
Every other one tells the customer about a thing they already did. This one
asks them to book again.

It used to go out to anyone who had ordinary reminders switched on — which
meant it was being sent by people who had never chosen to send it. That is
now fixed: it has its own switch, and that switch starts **off**. Nobody sends
it unless they decide to.

**There is currently no way to turn it on from the dashboard.** That is not an
oversight to route around — it is an open question, and it is question four
below.

## 3. Texting: the honest answer

**The product sends no texts. None.**

Here is what actually exists:

- **A screen where you write and keep text templates.** You can compose them,
  save them, edit them. It works.
- **Text buttons on a job and on a customer.** Pressing one opens the
  messaging app on your phone with the message already filled in.
- **A way to text several customers at once** from your client list — again,
  it opens your phone with all of them in the To field.

So texts do reach customers. **You send them, from your number, by pressing
send.** The product writes the message and hands it to you.

**What that means in practice, and none of it is obvious from the screen:**

- The product has no record that a text was ever sent. It can't show you a
  history, can't tell you if it was delivered, and can't stop a message going
  to someone who asked you to leave them alone.
- Replies go to your phone, not into the business.
- If you're not holding your phone, nothing goes out.
- There is no unsubscribe, because there is nothing central to unsubscribe
  from.

**The alternative — the platform texting from a shared business number — is
not built.** There is a supplier picked out and written down, and that is as
far as it goes. No account, no number, no code. It is a real piece of work,
not a switch.

## 4. Where nothing is sent at all

Walking through a customer's whole life with a detailer, these are the moments
where the product currently says nothing. They are listed because they are
missing, not because they should all exist.

| The moment | What happens now |
|---|---|
| **"I'm on my way"** | Nothing. This is the single most expected message in mobile service and there is no version of it, by email or text |
| **"I've finished"** | Nothing at the moment the work is done |
| **The day of the job** | The reminder goes out hours before by a setting; there is no separate morning-of message |
| **Their card was declined** | The customer is told nothing by the product. There is a message about a declined card, but it is about *your* subscription to the platform, not their payment for a job |
| **An invoice went unpaid** | Nothing chases it |
| **"How did we do?"** | **This one is built and is sitting idle.** The follow-up email asks for a review and offers a Google button and a Yelp button — but each button only appears if you have pasted that link in, and **only one of the sixteen businesses has.** For everyone else the email goes out as a plain thank-you |
| **Running late** | Nothing. No way to tell a customer you have slipped an hour |
| **Bad weather** | The product knows the forecast and shows it to you. It never tells the customer |
| **They haven't booked in a long time** | Nothing, unless a coating happens to be due |

**The first two are the ones a customer would notice.** "On my way" and
"finished" are the messages people expect from anyone who comes to their
house.

**And here is the part that makes them cheaper than they look.** The product
**already knows both of those moments** — it alerts *you* when a job is
starting and again when it is wrapping up. Those two alerts are in the push
list above. So this is not a gap in the product knowing when things happen.
**It is a gap in who gets told.** Whatever is decided about "on my way" and
"finished", nobody has to build the part that works out when to send them.

## 5. What the law actually asks for

**Plain terms, no advice.** This sets out what the rules ask and what the
product does. Whether that is good enough for your business is a question for
you and, if it matters, for a lawyer.

Everything splits into two kinds, and the split is what matters:

**Transactional** — a message about something the customer already did.
Booking confirmed. Moved. Cancelled. Reminder. Invoice. The customer asked for
the thing, so the message is expected.

**Marketing** — a message trying to get them to buy. "Your coating is due."
An offer. A campaign.

The line is about *purpose*, not tone. A friendly reminder is still
transactional. A polite "time to book again" is still marketing.

### Email

| What the rules ask | Where we stand |
|---|---|
| **A marketing email must say who sent it and give a real postal address** | **Already built, and stricter than the law.** There is a box for your business's postal address on your Business info screen. A campaign **will not send at all** without it — it stops and tells you to go and fill it in. **One of the sixteen businesses on the system has filled it in.** No other email carries an address, and none needs to |
| **A marketing email must have a working unsubscribe, honoured promptly** | **Built and working, for campaigns.** Every campaign carries a "Stop getting emails like this" link, the date someone opts out is recorded against them, and the next campaign skips them. **One customer out of forty-eight has used it**, so it is not theoretical |
| **Unsubscribing must not cut off a customer's transactional mail** | **Correct, and deliberate.** Someone who opts out still gets their confirmations, reminders, receipts and cancellations. Opting out of being sold to is not allowed to become a way of not being told when you are arriving |
| **You must not mislead in the subject line or the sender name** | Nothing in what goes out today looks like a problem |
| **Transactional email needs none of the above** | This is why the split matters. Every confirmation, reminder and receipt is clean on this point |

**But there is one email on the wrong side of that line, and it is worth
knowing before you answer question 3 or question 4.**

**"Your maintenance is due" is marketing, and it carries neither.** No
postal address and no unsubscribe link, and the opt-out list is not consulted
before it sends — so a customer who has pressed "stop getting emails like
this" would still receive it. The campaign email has all three; this one was
built alongside the ordinary reminders and never got them.

**Today that costs nothing, because the email is switched off everywhere and
there is no way to switch it on.** It matters the moment either of those
changes, which is exactly what questions 3 and 4 are about.

### Texting — and this is the strict one

**The rules for texting are much harder than for email, and the penalties are
per message.** The three that matter:

1. **You generally need permission before you text someone marketing, and it
   usually has to be written down in advance** — not assumed because they're a
   customer. Transactional texts about a booking they made sit differently.
2. **Every marketing text needs a way to stop it**, and STOP must work
   immediately and for good.
3. **You have to keep a record** of who agreed and who opted out.

**Where we stand: the product cannot do any of that, because it isn't the
sender.** There is no consent record, no opt-out list, and no send history —
there is nothing to keep them in. Right now every text is you, personally,
messaging a customer from your own phone, which is a very different thing from
a platform texting a list.

**The moment that changes is the moment the platform sends a text itself.**
All three of those become real requirements on day one, not later.

## 6. The questions that are yours

Each has the realistic options and what each costs. **Nothing here is
recommended and nothing is ranked.** These are the decisions, laid out.

### Question 1 — Should the platform send texts at all, or stay as it is?

| Option | What it costs you |
|---|---|
| **Leave it as it is** — you press send, from your phone | Nothing to build. Texts only go out when you're holding your phone, the business keeps no record, and nothing scales past you |
| **Platform sends from a shared number** | Real build: an account, a number, sending, delivery tracking, consent records, STOP handling. Brings every texting rule above into force immediately. Costs money per message |
| **Platform sends, but only about bookings** — never marketing | Same build, much lower risk, because booking messages are the kind the rules treat most gently. Gives up the selling use entirely |

### Question 2 — Which of the missing moments do you actually want?

Worth taking one at a time rather than as a list. For each: does a customer
expect it, and would you notice if it never went?

The ones most likely to matter: **"on my way"**, **"finished"**, **running
late**, and **chasing an unpaid invoice**. The first three are naturally
texts — they're time-critical and short. Which runs straight into question 1.

### Question 3 — Now that marketing email is allowed again, do you want any?

You've reversed the rule that said email is only ever receipts and reminders.
That doesn't decide whether you want marketing email — it just means you can.

| Option | What it costs you |
|---|---|
| **Still none** | Nothing to do. The rules stay irrelevant |
| **Campaigns** | **Almost nothing to build — this is the surprise.** The address box, the refusal to send without it, the unsubscribe link and the skip-list are all already there and working. The cost is **filling in your postal address once**, and deciding who goes on a list and why |
| **Only "your maintenance is due"** | **More work than campaigns, not less.** That email has no address, no unsubscribe link, and does not check the opt-out list. All three would have to be added to it before it could go to anybody |
| **Both** | The two above, together |

### Question 4 — Should a detailer be able to switch the maintenance email on?

Right now they can't — there's no control for it anywhere in the dashboard.
That was deliberate: the switch was left out while the rule said that email
must never go. **The rule has changed and the reason for leaving it out has
gone with it.**

| Option | What it costs you |
|---|---|
| **Leave it hidden** | Nobody sends it, including you. The message may as well not exist |
| **Add a switch, off by default** | Small on its own — but see section 5: that email has no address, no unsubscribe and does not check the opt-out list, so the switch should not be reachable until those exist. The real cost is those three, not the switch |
| **Add a switch and turn it on** | Sends a sales email by default from people who never asked to, which is exactly the situation that was just fixed — and today it would go out with no way to stop it |

### Question 5 — Do you want the review request switched on?

**This one is different from the others: it is already built, it is already
running, and it is doing nothing.** Every customer gets a thank-you email
after a job. If you have pasted in your Google and Yelp links, that email also
asks for a review and puts a button under it. If you have not, the ask is
silently left out — **one of the sixteen businesses has pasted them in.**

**Nothing is broken and no customer has seen a broken email.** The ask and the
buttons appear and disappear together, so nobody has ever received "a review
really helps us" with nothing to press.

| Option | What it costs you |
|---|---|
| **Paste both links in** | About thirty seconds. Every future thank-you email asks for a review with a button |
| **Paste one in** | Same, one button. The ask still appears |
| **Leave it** | The thank-you email stays a plain thank-you, as it is for fifteen of the sixteen |
| **Take the ask out of the product** | Work, and it removes a thing you may want later |

### Question 6 — Who is the sender?

Every customer email today goes out under the detailer's own business name.
That is almost certainly right. It is worth saying out loud because it decides
whose address goes in the footer, whose reputation suffers if it's marked as
spam, and who a customer replies to.

If the platform ever texts on a shared number, this gets harder: the number is
ours, the business name is theirs.

---

## 7. What is not in here

- **Nothing about what the messages say.** This is about which messages exist,
  not their wording.
- **Nothing about the platform's own emails to you** — billing, alerts. They
  work and they are a different conversation.
- **No legal advice.** Section 5 says what the rules ask and what the product
  does. The gap between those is visible on purpose, and closing it is a
  decision, not a fact.
